01 / The short version

A signal worth following.

A weekend search became a deeper investigation: isolate a faint star’s light, check the repeating dip, then try to explain it away. This is the record of what survived.

What we see

A dip about two hours long, repeating across four years of Kepler observations. The lost light points to KIC 5689121, a cool red dwarf in a crowded field.

What it suggests

A close-orbiting, sub-Neptune-sized planet. It would receive about five times Earth’s sunlight. Its atmosphere, mass, and composition have not been measured.

What is still open

The latest false-positive model gives about 7%, above the 1.5% validation threshold. Most of that risk is a planet orbiting an unresolved companion instead of this star.

A year of just 13.77 days. The fitted period is 13.769448 ± 0.000065 days. The depth depends on the extraction and baseline: approximately 1,830–2,430 ppm in the separated star’s light.

How do you see a planet without seeing it?

If an orbit crosses our line of sight, a planet can block a small fraction of its star’s light. We measure the brightness over time, then align many orbits to make that small dip easier to distinguish from noise. A dip alone is not proof of a planet: eclipsing stars and blended light can mimic one.

Why 70 transits in one result and 74 in another?

The original vetter’s stricter quality cut kept 70 events, 22 in reserved quarters. The later display export measures 74 events, including 23 in reserved quarters. “Every event” shows that 74-event export. The folded display uses the saved full light curve; the stricter count is not a mask on this display. Selection notes ↗

The holdout test: predict observations you did not search

The initial search reserved the last four quarters. Its fixed ephemeris still recovered a 5.84σ signal in aperture photometry, with 22 accepted events and no single event dominating. The newer full-baseline fit uses all quarters; displaying it is not another blind holdout test. Read the aperture recheck ↗

04 / Trying to explain it away

Why this candidate
survived the checks.

The hard part is not finding a dip. It is working out which star dimmed, and whether something else could reproduce the same pattern.

✓

It repeats in reserved data

The original frozen ephemeris recovered the dip in the last four reserved quarters at 5.84σ in aperture photometry. Audit ↗

✓

Kepler-169 does not explain it

Almost the same period, but never the same transit: the nearest Kepler-169 e event is 67 hours away. The periods differ by 32σ. Measurement ↗

✓

The brighter neighbour stays steady

KIC 5689116 shows 24 ± 41 ppm at the candidate’s schedule. It would need roughly 1,100 ppm to explain the observed signal. Measurement ↗

✓

Extra light dilutes the dip

Larger apertures collect more neighbour light and make the dip shallower—just as a transit on the target predicts. The dilution model fits with p = 0.22. Measurement ↗

✓

The pixels point to this star

Raw-pixel centroid shifts strongly favour the target over the bright neighbour (Δχ² = 934). Other resolved Gaia sources within 30″ fit much worse. Measurement ↗

✓

The shape is compatible with a planet

Odd and even events agree; there is no convincing secondary eclipse. The fitted stellar density agrees with a small red dwarf. These checks constrain alternatives; they do not independently confirm a planet.

?

A hidden companion remains possible

A planet around an unresolved bound star could still mimic the signal. High-resolution imaging and a companion constraint are needed.

?

Novelty is provisional

No match was found in the checked catalogue snapshots. Live TOI/CTOI and literature checks were incomplete in the validation environment; absence from a snapshot does not prove first discovery.

05 / The remaining uncertainty

Promising does not
mean proven.

TRICERATOPS compares possible planetary and stellar explanations. With the available inputs, the candidate does not cross the threshold for statistical validation.

6.9%

Mean modelled false-positive probability. Four runs span 5.8–8.9%. This is a conditional model result, not a direct measurement of “the chance we found a planet.”

All run outputs ↗ · 1,000,000 draws per run

The unresolved question is the host.

The largest remaining alternative is still a planet—but one orbiting a close, unresolved companion star. That would change the inferred size and the host assignment.

Planet on target, target single (TP)75.0%
Planet on target, bound companion (PTP)17.2%
Planet on bound companion (STP)6.8%
Remaining scenarios~1.0%

Scenario shares from the baseline model. Not all of the remaining ~1% counts as a false positive under the model’s definitions.

Imaging has not been obtained. The “what if” result uses a hypothetical adaptive-optics contrast curve. Even then, its mean FPP is about 2.7%, still above 1.5%. Companions closer than roughly 0.1″ (~30 AU) remain hard to exclude.

What the probability includes—and what it does not

The offline model covers on-target and unresolved-blend scenarios using a Gaia-derived field population. Resolved neighbours were not passed into TRICERATOPS, so its reported NFPP = 0 is bookkeeping, not evidence of zero neighbour risk. Those sources were tested separately with their light curves and the raw pixels. Gaia RUWE = 0.984 helps constrain some companions, but this version of TRICERATOPS does not incorporate it. The 8.7236-day dip was recovered blind in both halves of the data. An early ephemeris predicted the late transit 89 cycles later, within its uncertainty. The depth is 401 ± 30 ppm and the duration about one hour.

But its grazing shape also fits an eclipsing binary bound to the star. TRICERATOPS gives FPP 55.6–57.9%, with about 42% in the dominant bound-binary scenario. Reconnaissance spectra and multi-colour transits would be more revealing than imaging alone. Official-PRF TESS pixel reanalysis was blocked in the validation environment.

Search noise can look persuasive, too.

Inverting the light curve turns real dips into brightening events. Running the same search on that inverted data measures how often the pipeline finds a transit-like pattern anyway.

Two of 1,736 matched source searches reached comparable detection strength. About two such null-level signals are expected at this survey size. The case for KIC 5689121 rests on the follow-up vetting, not detection strength alone.

1,736 source searches cover 1,648 unique KICs. Independent check ↗

Coverage, positive controls and incomplete work

Known planets including LHS 3844 b and Kepler-138 c/d were recovered as positive controls. Earlier inverted TESS searches produced five first-screen passes in 100 trials. Injection-recovery work in the historical TESS extension is incomplete; no complete survey sensitivity or occurrence-rate claim is made.

08 / From a weekend idea to a testable claim

What happens next
needs more than code.

The next useful observation would constrain a close companion. Re-running the same light curve cannot resolve every remaining explanation.

Three ways to tighten the case

  1. High-resolution imaging. Look for a close companion with adaptive optics, then use a real contrast curve.
  2. Companion modelling. Combine imaging and Gaia astrometry in a companion constraint, then rerun the false-positive calculation.
  3. A multi-band transit. Check whether the depth changes with colour, as a differently coloured hidden host could predict.

A radial-velocity mass would be difficult for this faint star. Statistical validation is the more realistic next milestone.

How the work unfolded

Kuber’s account starts with an idea on an evening run. Codex helped with the initial search; much of the subsequent hunt was carried out with ChatGPT Work. The repository preserves the code, results, later checks, and revisions.

  1. 09 Oct

    TESS search begins across nearby K and M dwarfs, with checks for binary-like signals and contamination.

  2. 10 Oct

    A Kepler Bonus background-star search identifies KIC 5689121. Aperture rechecks and the evidence repository follow.

  3. 11 Oct

    An extended search and independent validation add centroid, dilution, holdout and false-positive tests.

Independent checking matters. A separate analysis re-derived key measurements with its own code. It confirmed the main patterns while exposing method-dependent depth and noise estimates. This is an additional computational audit, not external astronomical validation. Read the audit ↗
